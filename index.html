Islet Grid Simulator

One switch design, the Islet node, sits at every home, every transformer, and every feeder section. Cheap chips in each node measure the power and decide locally in milliseconds. Regional computers merge neighborhoods into islands in seconds, and one central computer handles the base, the loop to the second substation, and the return to normal. Pick a scenario and watch the rules hold.

Tier 1 · every node · ~10 msNode chipMeasures volts, amps, frequency. Trips on faults and grid loss with no outside help.
Tier 2 · every transformer · ~1 sTransformer coordinatorBuilds the 15-home island over its own wires (powerline signal), even if radios fail.
Tier 3 · per region · ~5 sRegional computerMerges transformer islands along the feeder and shares energy between them.
Tier 4 · one per utility · ~10 sCentral computerRuns the base link, the loop to substation B, and reconnecting to the grid.

The product

Inside an Islet home node

The home version fits between the utility meter and its socket, like the meter collars utilities already approve. The transformer, section and base versions use the same control board on bigger switches, so every node in the grid speaks the same rules.

Close permit: try to close the switch

Every node runs this check before it closes, whether the request comes from the central computer or from someone pulling the handle. Opening never needs permission.

    Night schedule stored on every node

    The clock chip keeps time on the supercapacitor through outages, and the schedule lives on the node, so it works even with every link down. Caps apply only when a home is on island power. On the grid, nothing is capped. When storage is short, the regional computer can switch every node to a stricter plan: sleep caps all night, 0.5 kW per home from 9 PM to sunrise.

    Every open problem, and the rule that handles it

    ProblemHow Islet handles itScenario that tests it
    Many batteries fighting to set the voltageOne leader per island (largest healthy battery or the base). Every battery must be grid-forming and follow the shared sharing rule. If the leader fails, the next one takes over in about 20 ms.Leader battery fails
    Batteries can't push enough current to blow fusesEvery node compares current in and out of its section, so a fault shows up as a mismatch, not a big current. Backup: inverters add a small harmonic signal during faults that relays can read with no communication. A test pulse checks for faults before any close.Tree hits the line · Comms cut
    Lineworkers hit by power from an islandA padlock on the manual handle physically blocks closing. Software never re-energizes a locked section. A grounded line looks like a short and trips instantly.Crew lockout
    Radio or fiber links go downHomes with batteries island themselves. The transformer coordinator still builds 15-home islands over its own wires using powerline signals.Comms cut
    Hackers switching off thousands of homesNo remote command can disconnect a home that the grid is still powering; only local measurements can. Commands must be signed and are rate-limited. Hidden radios are screened out in hardware audits.Cyberattack
    Switches flapping on and offA switch holds 15 s after any move, and new homes are added only against the lowest solar output seen in the last minute.Cloud flicker (toggle anti-flap to compare)
    An island running out of energyThe lead battery lowers frequency as it drains. Below 59.75 Hz, homes drop AC and EV charging automatically, and the regional computer merges the island with a stronger neighbor or the base.Batteries run low
    Base at the end of a single lineThe base connects to a second substation through a normally-open switch, powers itself first, and lends only its surplus.Substation A fails · Regional blackout
    A heat-wave night with no sunPartly. Each node's clock caps its home by time of night, down to a 0.2 kW essentials floor when storage runs low. With 12 working batteries, all 90 homes stay on until about midnight on the clock schedule, or about 1:20 AM with sleep caps all night, instead of about 10:20 PM. The essentials floor lets the island restart at sunrise. The rest of the night still needs more storage.Heat-wave night (switch the night plan to compare)
    Reconnecting without damageThe loop switch opens before the main feed closes, so two substations are never tied together. Every close passes the IEEE 1547 sync limits.Grid returns

    What this proof of concept shows

    Shown here

    The control rules work together. In every scenario, islands form, faults stay isolated, locked sections stay dead, forged commands change nothing, and no close happens outside the sync limits. The tiers degrade safely when communication drops.

    Not shown yet

    The electrical physics. Power flow here is simplified to capacity and load. Real fault currents, inverter stability in the first few milliseconds, radio delays and three-phase balance need a circuit simulator and hardware.

    Next proof steps

    Model one feeder in OpenDSS with these rules. Then build three bench nodes from a microcontroller, metering IC and relay on a 240 V test rig. Then pilot one transformer with a utility partner.
